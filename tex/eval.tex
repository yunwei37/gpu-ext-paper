\subsection{\sys{} Performance}
We evaluated \sys{} by comparing its end-to-end performance against a native implementation of the same workloads. 
In this comparison, both systems utilized the same input data and computational tasks, but the native version excluded the overhead of persistent kernels, shared memory synchronization, and PTX injection. 
Our measurements focused on overall throughput and total processing time from the initial data transfer to the final result retrieval. 

In small-scale scenarios with relatively light input batches, we observed that \sys{} introduces a modest overhead due to the persistent kernel’s initialization and JIT compilation steps. 
This overhead is offset by a significant reduction in kernel launch frequency, which becomes increasingly beneficial as task size or complexity grows. For larger inputs and more irregular workloads, \sys{} consistently outperformed or closely matched the native implementation in total execution time. 
The ability to avoid repeated kernel launches, particularly under streaming or dynamically generated tasks, yielded lower latency and more consistent GPU utilization.

\subsection{Shared Memory Performance}
We evaluated the different shared memory implementations on three separate servers.
To characterize the performance of our shared‐memory implementation, we ran a suite of microbenchmarks on three distinct server configurations.  
Each experiment measured two key metrics: raw shared‐memory bandwidth (GB/s) and one‐way access latency (ns), using both CPU‐only and GPU‐access patterns. On each platform, we allocated a 1 GiB shared region, then measured: \textbf{GPU→CPU} and \textbf{CPU→GPU} throughput via pinned‐buffer kernels and host‐pinned memcpy.  

We observed that Servers A and B achieved peak CPU‐to‐GPU bandwidths of 12–18 GB/s over PCIe, with one‐way latencies of 8–15 µs for cross‐domain barriers. 
By contrast, Server C’s unified coherent fabric delivered over 100 GB/s sustained throughput and reduced round‐trip latencies to under 500 ns, \textit{demonstrating the dramatic benefits of hardware‐coherent shared memory for live GPU instrumentation.}

\subsection{Use Case Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsubsection{MemTrace}\label{sec:eval_memtrace}
We evaluate MemTrace on both synthetic microbenchmarks and real-world GPU kernels to quantify its accuracy, overhead, and diagnostic utility. 
First, using a custom bank-conflict microbenchmark that repeatedly issues strided loads with varying strides, we verify that MemTrace correctly reconstructs per-warp memory‐access patterns and bank‐conflict counts. 
Across 1M memory transactions, MemTrace reports conflict rates within 2\% of ground truth obtained via hardware counters, demonstrating high fidelity.

To assess overhead, in \Cref{fig:memtrace}, we instrument two common CUDA kernels—matrix multiplication (1024×1024 tiles) and sparse‐matrix–vector multiply (SpMV) on a 10M-edge graph, a dense matrix‐multiply (compute‐bound), sparse‐matrix–vector multiply (memory‐bound), 1D stencil (balanced). 
In all cases, MemTrace incurs under 3\% end-to-end runtime overhead compared to uninstrumented execution, with peak PTX‐injection latency below 50 µs per kernel launch. 
Finally, we showcase debugging of a tiled convolution kernel from a popular deep‐learning framework: MemTrace pinpoints an unexpected bank‐conflict hotspot in the inner tile loop, guiding a simple padding fix that improves throughput by 12\%.

\subsubsection{Fair Share Scheduling}\label{sec:eval_fair_sched}

We evaluate Fair Share Scheduling in multi‐tenant scenarios where several GPU kernels with differing resource demands (compute‐bound, memory‐bound, and mixed) run concurrently on the same device. 
We compare three policies: (1) a naïve FIFO queue, (2) a static proportional‐share allocator that divides blocks evenly by tenant, and (3) \sys’s dynamic Fair Share Scheduler, which continuously measures per‐block execution times via injected PTX trampolines and reassigns block‐launch quotas to equalize effective progress across tenants.

Experiments use a mix of 2 workloads: ResNet‐18 inference, and a graph‐analytics BFS kernel. Each tenant submits blocks in fixed batches of 256; under Fair Share Scheduling, the host scheduler recalculates each tenant’s next batch size every 10 ms to target equalized completion ratios. We quantify fairness using Jain’s fairness index:
\[
  F = \frac{\bigl(\sum_{i=1}^M x_i\bigr)^2}{M\,\sum_{i=1}^M x_i^2}\!,
\]
where \(x_i\) is the throughput achieved by tenant \(i\) and \(M=3\).

\Cref{fig:plot_cuda_scheduler} presents a side-by-side comparison of our three scheduling policies—Baseline FIFO, Static Proportional, and Fair Share—in terms of both end-to-end application runtime (blue bars, left axis) and average per-block launch latency (orange bars, right axis). Under the naïve FIFO policy, the total execution time climbs to roughly 205 s and the average kernel-launch latency sits just below 1 ms, reflecting unregulated queuing delays and resource contention among co-running tenants. Switching to a static proportional-share allocator immediately cuts the total runtime by about 35 \%, down to 130 s, and reduces average launch latency to roughly 0.6 ms. However, the large error bar on the static-schedule’s latency measurement indicates high variability from run to run, likely due to imbalances in how fixed block quotas interact with each tenant’s particular compute- versus memory-bound behavior.

When we enable our dynamic Fair Share Scheduler, we see a further reduction in both metrics: total runtime falls to approximately 122 s (a further 6 \% speedup), and average launch latency tightens to around 0.5 ms. 
Moreover, the error bars for Fair Share are noticeably smaller on both bars, demonstrating that adaptive quota adjustments not only boost average throughput but also dramatically improve performance stability. 
In short, Fair Share Scheduling delivers near–ideal fairness across heterogeneous workloads while simultaneously minimizing the overhead of repeated kernel launches—outperforming both FIFO and static proportional sharing in overall efficiency and consistency.

\subsubsection{ML Green: CPU–GPU Collaborative Inference}\label{sec:eval_ml_green}
For the CPU–GPU collaborative inference use case, we integrate \sys into a ResNet-50 image-classification pipeline and a LLAMA2-based question-answering pipeline, running on an H100 Server backed by CXL memory. We compare (1) naïve static CPU–GPU splitted inference, (2) optimized online profiling and LRU or LFU caching policy, and (3) \sys’s full CPU and full GPU offloading.

\paragraph{Static CPU–GPU splitted inference}
In \Cref{fig:slicing-percentage}, if we offload more of the ResNet and LLaMA2 workloads onto the GPU—which unlike the tiny MNIST network consisting of deep convolutional or transformer layers with high compute‐to‐memory ratios—the device’s massive parallelism and specialized tensor hardware begin to pay off in spades. 
In the ResNet experiments, moving even a few of the earlier convolutional blocks onto the GPU drives down the worst‐case slice latency by upwards of 15–20\%, and as we increase the GPU slice count into the mid‐single‐digits, the tail latency falls to roughly 70–80\% of the CPU‐only baseline. 
This improvement arises because the GPU handles the large matrix‐multiply and convolution operations far more efficiently than the host, amortizing the cost of each kernel launch across thousands of parallel threads. 
Likewise, for LLaMA2’s transformer‐style attention and feed‐forward layers, distributing work across multiple CUDA kernels exploits the device’s warp‐level scheduling and tensor cores to whittle the maximum slice execution time down to 60–70\% relative to the purely CPU‐bound case. 
These gains are not unbounded: once we reach high GPU occupancy and saturate the global memory interface, further slicing yields diminishing returns. Kernel launch overhead, register pressure, and contention for shared resources begin to counterbalance the benefits of finer granularity, producing a performance plateau beyond roughly 20–25 GPU slices. 
Taken together, these results underscore that model size, layer complexity, and compute‐to‐memory characteristics must all be factored into any static slice‐and‐offload strategy: small, lightweight models may actually regress under GPU offload, whereas large, data‐parallel networks stand to gain dramatically—provided the slicing granularity is tuned to the GPU’s execution and memory‐access profile.

\Cref{fig:heatmap,fig:heatmap1} depict end-to-end inference latencies for the 32‐layer LLaMA2 model as a function of the CPU–GPU split point. Each heatmap cell represents the total inference time when layers 1…(start–1) execute on the host CPU, layers start…end on the GPU, and the remaining layers on the CPU again; invalid splits (end < start) are left blank. On both servers, latencies steadily decrease as more layers are offloaded—culminating in the bottom‐right cell (start = 1, end = 32), which corresponds to the pure-GPU case. Indeed, the fastest GPU-only run clocks in at 0.323 s, slightly outperforming the best two‐layer split (0.347 s). Server A incurs overall latencies of approximately 9.12–9.15 s for mixed CPU/GPU execution, whereas Server B’s superior GPU drives times down to roughly 0.5–8 s. Beyond roughly 20–25 offloaded layers, further splitting yields only marginal gains, reflecting kernel-launch overheads and memory‐interface saturation that offset the benefits of finer‐grained offload.

\paragraph{Optimized online profiling and LRU or LFU caching policy}
During the execution of a LLAMA2 7B‐based application across CPU and GPU, \sys continuously collects lightweight telemetry—per‐layer execution times, data‐transfer latencies, and cache‐hit statistics—via the host‐side profiler and in‐kernel PTX trampolines. At regular intervals (e.g.\ every 100 inferences), these measurements feed an adaptive optimizer that selects the split point minimizing end‐to‐end latency while balancing CPU load and GPU throughput. 
To avoid repeating the full profiling cycle on every batch, \sys maintains a small “partition cache” in the shared memory region: each entry maps a chosen layer index to its precompiled PTX injection snippet and the corresponding performance delta. When cache capacity is reached, entries are evicted according to either a least‐recently‐used (LRU) or least‐frequently‐used (LFU) policy, ensuring that the most effective partition decisions remain resident. 
By reusing cached splits and kernels, \sys reduces profiling overhead to under 2 \% of total runtime. 
In practice, this online profiling plus smart eviction strategy yields consistent throughput gains of 10-12 \% over static partitioning, even as input characteristics or model behavior evolve at runtime.

\paragraph{Full CPU Full GPU offloading}
Figure \ref{fig:power_vs_throughput} overlays our one-day renewable generation profile with the end-to-end token‐processing throughput of two inference pipelines (Tok1 stands for Full CPU Full GPU offloading and Tok2 stands for GPU frequency scaling) on December 31, 2015. 
On the left vertical axis we plot solar (orange) and wind (blue) power in kilowatts; on the right axis we show tokens-per-second for each pipeline (green and red curves). Between midnight and \~08:00 both solar and wind outputs remain near zero, and token throughput sits at a steady $\approx$0.8 tokens/s. 
After sunrise solar generation climbs smoothly, peaking at \~12.5 kW \~15:00, before tapering off to zero by early evening. Wind exhibits a similar midday rise—albeit with slightly more variability—rising from negligible overnight levels to roughly 12 kW at the same peak hour. In lockstep with available renewable power, both Tok1 and Tok2 ramp from their nighttime baseline up to about 1.35–1.40 tokens/s at the generation peak, then fall back as the sun sets and winds calm. Throughout the day Tok2 maintains a small but consistent advantage ($\approx$0.05 tokens/s) over Tok1. This tight coupling between energy availability and processing throughput underscores how full-offload scheduling can exploit fluctuating renewable supply to maximize inference performance without exceeding the instantaneous power budget.
